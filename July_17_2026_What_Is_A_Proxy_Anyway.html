The LAN Switch Is Hardly Thinking

July 16, 2026

What is a proxy server? The mildly informed may think that it’s a magical virtual wall one hides behind So that the feds think you’re downloading a ripped copy of Sisterhood Of The Travelling Pants from a desktop in Lithuania, rather than Anytown, USA in the comfort of your double-wide living room- where you watch all of your shameful girly guilty pleasures. Embarrassing admissions aside, that doesn’t scratch the surface of what a proxy server is, or what it does.

To summarize neatly, a Proxy server is a server that acts on behalf of another device. It rests right in the middle of the client and the applications they mean to handle. To summarize it less neatly, it is an intermediary system (sitting in between) that receives network requests from a device and forwards them to another system on the initial device’s behalf. This means that rather than communicate directly with the destination server, it asks an intermediate device to do so for you. To better sum this up, imagine being in the third grade again. You're emotionally handling your first or second crush- but like most third-graders, you’re nervous about addressing it directly. Let's say one of your friends kind of talks to this person’s clique. You ask them to raise the question to the person because you’re in the third grade—and you have no idea how socialization is supposed to work. At any rate, you also get indirectly rejected, so you go home and watch Sisterhood Of The Travelling Pants, And demolish a whole gallon of Rocky Road. That is how a proxy works.

A company or school could use a proxy to block prohibited websites, scan downloads, or enforce acceptable-use policies. It isn’t all shadow work and ts versions of feminine coming-of-age stories. But all of this kind of refers to what a forward proxy does. That isn’t the only kind of proxy.

A reverse proxy, conversely, represents servers rather than the clients reaching those servers. Bet you didn’t see that one coming. It will typically sit in front of one or more web servers. It receives requests from users and decides which internal server ought to be handling the request. Reverse proxies are more commonly used for load-balancing, caching, TLS termination, and hiding internal system structures from otherwise prying eyes. To any of us on the user end, the reverse proxy appears to be the actual website, even if it may be distributing traffic across a plethora of backend servers.

Use cases beyond bootlegged copies of The Nanny Diaries include things like Access control, that is...blocking websites, file types, or consistent policy violations. A proxy can also be used to monitor traffic. It can record requests for troubleshooting or security investigations it can also be used, as mentioned previously, to filter content based on detection of malware or perhaps inappropriate content in general. It can even be used for TLS termination- which i have already mentioned, and I'm sure that’s the point this is all kind of leaning into.

A proxy shouldn't be regarded as a sole source of security or privacy. The operator of a proxy may be able to view or log traffic. Even more than that, an improperly configured proxy can become a new vector of vulnerability. Proxies are not magic- but they’re pretty cool in this intern’s humble opinion.
