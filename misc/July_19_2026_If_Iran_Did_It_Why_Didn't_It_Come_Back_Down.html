If Iran Did It, Why Didn't It Come Back Down?

July 19, 2026

I was standing at the pump, being angry about two days ago- like most Americans have become familiar with doing and initially, I gave it no further thought. I drove to work yesterday, using that same tank of gas. It wasn't until my co-workers began talking that a thought occured to me. They seemed to almost collectively agree that Trump was to blame for the gas prices. This is an argument all of us are familiar with hearing and it comes from both sides of the aisle. You're familiar with those stickers people leave on pumps... a picture of either president on a sticker with a caption or speech bubble that says "I did that." with a finger pointing at the price, depending on where said vandal places it. But I thought about it and asked a simple enough question out loud; "how did Trump change gas prices?" I'll be the first to admit I didn't have the foresight to ask this question during the Biden administration where it may not have always been merited. As you might imagine, the room got quiet. I followed up with something to the effect of "I thought publicly traded companies set their own prices." more silence.

So who is responsible? well first, let's go back a little bit. Gas prices went up after the conflict with Iran disrupted oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz. This isn't a controversial thing to say. To that end, I get it. it isn't lost on me or anyone else that the president is pushing for conflict in Iran. Oil became more expensive, gasoline predictably followed in tow. Americans paid more at the pump. Not a complicated thought process to follow. This explanation cuts the chain of command a little too short. When crude oil prices later returned to pre-conflict levels, gasoline didn't dollow. On July 3rd, Reuters reported that benchmark crude prices returned to prewar levels while American gasoline remained approximately 22 percent more expensive than it had been, before the conflict. [4]

So where is the difference going? this isn't a partisan question. It isn't an accusation that any given President is responsible for the whims of oil companies or gasoline retailers. It is a basic question of finance: If the underlying commodity (in this instance, crude oil) returned to its previous price, why did the finished product (the gasoline you pump into your vehicle) remain substantially more expensive?

There is a name for this behavior: "rockets and feathers." Gasoline prices have this tendency to rocket upward, then feather back down a lot more slowly than they had risen. An FTC economist studied daily and weekly gasoline prices and the finding in the after math was that, in general, retail prices rose morre than four times as quickly as they fell.[1] The specific amount of Asymmetry varies by location, brand, and given year but the overall pattern remains measurable. This means that generally speaking, consumers like you can end up paying increased prices at the pump even if the original cause has since vanished. The industry can offer explanations involving inventory replacement, refinery operations, and even anticipated future costs, along with a slough of other usual suspects. Some of those explanations can be valid individual cases- fair. But none of that changes the basic conclusion that when the prices drop more slowly than the costs, the gap becomes revenue somewhere along the supply chain.

On the third of July, The justice department and FTC announced that they were keepign an eye on petroleum markets, and enourages state Attorneys general to investigate any potential price manipulation, collusion, or consumer-protection violations.[3] The agencies specifically argued that not enough of the reduction in crude pricing was reaching customers at the pump. It would appear I'm not the only one asking these questions. I just find it a little strange that A lot of people who claim to be for the individual- socialists, democrats, even progresssive republicans on an individual basis- refuse to point the finger any further than the president. this can only result in one of two things as I see it. Either the American government is at the complete and total mercy of oil companies which is an unthinkable violation of our rights and principles...Or the president has an amount of control over otherwise necessary utilities that puts the people at the complete mercy of whichever party or individual even- who happens to be in office during any given era. Too many people aren't seeing it, but this goes far beyond a frustrated driver muttering obscenities next to a pump. Federal regulators gave a full throated acknowledgement that the drop in crude prices was not being bassed on to the consumer sufficiently.

The announcement did nothign to publicly identify companies being subpoenaed, federal industry investigations, or any specific suspected agreements. All it amounted to was essentially warnings and a request for state-level action. This is an important distinctions. Knowing a suspicious price pattern exists is not equivalent to taking meaningful action. We the angry consumers want to know where all of this money is going.

We could then tell ourselves, if we really wanted to, that gasoline was still expensive because the refineries were sitting idle or maybe experiencing a drop in production. The most recent federal figures do not support this conjecture though. For the week ending july 10, American refineries operated at 96.2% of their available capacity. together they processed approximately 17.1 million barrels of crude oil per day. Gasoline production alone averaged approximately 9.6 million barrels per day. [2] At the same time, while refining a phenomenal amount of oil, gasoline inventories had fallen 210.5 million barrels. This however, is only 8% below the 5 year seasonal average. that puts us 22.4 million barrels lower than the smae week in 2025. [2]

Reuters reported that gasoline inventories had dipped just over 42 million barrels since February 27. this put the country at its lowest gasoline reserve for that time of year since 2012. Part of the decline had to do with an increase of demand from international customers. America had sold a record quantity to the internaitonal community. [5] This makes the question a little more specific, considering record exports resulted in just a hiccup in national quantity. If domestic inventories were aready shrinking, why did we experience record fuel exports coupled with the highest margin products priotized during a period of severe domestic price pressure? Exporting fuel is fine. collecting a profit is expected. neither of those dictated that the results should be immune to public scrutiny.

A refinery doesn't just earn money from the price of crude oil. It also comes from the difference between crude it purchases measured against the gasoline, diesel, and even jet fuel that it sells. The approximation of that difference is called a "crack spread." On July 16, the benchmark 3-2-1 crack spread reached a record high of $69.99 per barrel. The gasoline spread specifically was approximately $59 per barrel while diesel's spread exceeded $91 per barrel. [5]

So what are the quarterly eaernings going to show? presently, they haven't been released yet. Analysts surveyed before reports projected that ExxonMobil was to report approximately $15.9 billion in adjusted net income. this is more than triple the first-quarter result. Chevron similarly is slated to report approximately $9.9 billion of the same. which is also more than triple the first quarter result. The average second-quarter gasoline refining spreads are to be approximately $25 per barrel, which is approximately $16 higher than in the first quarter. and average diesel spreads will sit at $45 per barrel, which puts it about $15 higher than during Q1. [4] Keep in mind, these are forecasts. not results. but its worth keeping an eye on. Reutersspecifically says that they are estimates rather than completed earnings.

So how much profit came from refining versus profit from crude? how much did companies earn from exporting fuel while American inventories declined? How much cash went toward increasing supply, and how much went toward dividends and stock buybacks? Did executives inform their investors that geopolitical instability expandid margin? A profitable quarter alone doesn't prove price-fixing was taking place. But if the industry records an exceptional profit increase while crude prices briefly return to pre-conflict levels, and gasoline doesn't, there is no reason why a serious investigation isn't being demanded, as far as I can tell.

Competitors are prohibited from agreeing to raise prices, limit output, divide markets, or suppress competition. direct coordination could lead to criminal antitrust charges if prosecuted correctly. The difficulty comes in proving coordination occoured. Companies operating in the same industries generally watch the same benchmarks. This means they receive the same market information, and use the same pricing models. they COULD move in parallel without openly admitting to any kind of agreement. so every participant has a solid alibi; They simply followed the global benchmark. so who sets the benchmark? who is orchestrating this? Because what we have currently is a system set up- seemingly intentionally- to allow for every layer to point a finger at another layer.

Political commentary typically ends with one of two claims; either that Iran is responsible for gas prices, or the president is responsible for gas prices. neither even comes close to scratching the surface of adequately describing what happened. Iranian conflict- regardless of who you blame- can explain the initial disruption. Presidential decisions can contribute to global conditions indirectly. fine. Neither insanely simplified explanation gives us any indication as to why gasoline remained elevated, even as crude prices briefly returned to prewar levels. It doesn't explain why margins expanded so quickly, or why domestic inventireos were only slightly depleted while exports surged. IT certainly tells us nothing about who is retaining the money during the slow decline.

The EIA reported the national average retail price for regular gasoline being $3.855 per gallon as of July 13. This compared to $3.130 one week earlier. [2] Blaming or praising the president or a country takes the crosshairs off of the target. It allows every private company involved to evade any kind of meaningful quesitoning. What portion of this price was necessary to cover actual cost, and what amount of it becmae additional margin because consumers had no price but to pay outlandish prices? These aren't accusations. they're questions. The public deserves to be better-informed. especially given that I had to write a damned essay to summarize the flow of responsibility from one end to the other- where the usual public conversation is "the president did this" at any given time. Maybe eggs weren't directly Biden's fault. Maybe the farmers were exploiting the system the same way. In all cases of consumer strain, we're the ones who suffer. Who gains from our suffering is an important question which seems never to be answered. Q2 shows that temporary conflict produced astronomical lasting gains for the fuel industry. The public should not settle on "this is Trump's fault" or "This is Iran's fault." left and right. let's all come together. let's form a united front against these companies who clearly see us as profitable and useful idiots.
